Chapter XXXIII

Phrases

Six kinds of Phrases

Phrases are classified by the part of speech whose work they do in the sentence.

—
যে Phrase বাক্যে Noun-এর কাজ করে, তাকে Noun Phrase বলে।
  • To ride well requires practice.
  • His going home is uncertain.
  • He likes to play tennis.
  • He enjoys walking by the river-side.
—
যে Phrase বাক্যে Adjective-এর কাজ করে অর্থাৎ কোনো Noun বা Pronoun-কে বিশেষিত করে, তাকে Adjective Phrase বলে।
  • He gave me a chain of gold (= a gold chain).
  • He is without fear (= fearless).
  • It is the deed of a hero (= a heroic deed).
  • I am free from blame (= blameless).
  • He is a man of fame (= a famous man).
  • This is a cycle made in England (= an English cycle).
  • The house built long ago (= the old house) fell down.

Not every Adjective Phrase can be replaced by a single Adjective:

  • The man in the street knows it.
  • The tree in front of my house has been cut down.
  • A boy desirous of winning the prize must work hard.
Note — The same phrase may be Noun or Adjectival depending on use. Walking by the river-side is a Noun phrase in "He enjoys walking by the river-side," but Adjectival in "The boy walking by the river-side is my brother."
—
যে Phrase বাক্যে Adverb-এর কাজ করে, তাকে Adverbial Phrase বলে।
  • He was at the place (= there).
  • He worked with care (= carefully).
  • He came at a great speed (= quickly).
  • I want it at this moment (= now).
  • You can get it in all places (= everywhere).
  • He will come before very long (= soon).
  • He fell down on this spot (= here).

Not all Adverbial Phrases have a one-word Adverb equivalent:

  • He came to see me.
  • We fell from the tree.
  • Come into the garden.
  • This must be done at any price.
  • He lives on a small income.
Note — The same phrase may be Adjectival or Adverbial depending on use. In "He is in the room," in the room is Adverbial; in "The men in the room rushed out," it is Adjectival.
—
যে Phrase বাক্যে Preposition-এর কাজ করে, তাকে Prepositional Phrase বলে।
  • He stood in front of me.
  • She is at the point of death.
  • He persevered in the teeth of all opposition.
  • He could not come on account of illness.
Note — The whole phrase in front of in "He stood in front of me" is Adverbial as a unit (qualifying stood), yet the phrase itself works prepositionally.
—
  • Come as soon as you can.
  • No sooner had he left the place than the fire broke out.
  • Not only he but his brother also were present.
—
  • What a pity!
  • By Jove!
  • Good heavens!
Note — Some grammarians use the term "verbal phrase" for group verbs (He laughs at me. He looks down upon me.) — but since these contain a finite verb, the term "phrase" strictly does not apply to them.
Chapter XXXIV

Clauses

In "As he is ill, he cannot come," both "As he is ill" and "he cannot come" are small sentences (each with its own subject and predicate) forming part of the bigger sentence. Such small sentences are called clauses.

একগুচ্ছ শব্দ, যার নিজস্ব Subject ও Finite Verb আছে, কিন্তু যা একটি বৃহত্তর বাক্যের অংশ মাত্র, তাকে Clause বলে।

Remember: a finite verb is the main mark of a clause — the number of clauses in a sentence equals the number of finite verbs in it.

Clauses are of three kinds: Principal, Subordinate (Dependent), and Co-ordinate.

যে Clause একা দাঁড়িয়ে সম্পূর্ণ অর্থ প্রকাশ করে এবং যার উপর Subordinate Clause নির্ভরশীল, তাকে Principal Clause বলে।
যে Clause অন্য একটি Clause-এর উপর নির্ভরশীল এবং একা সম্পূর্ণ অর্থ প্রকাশ করতে পারে না, তাকে Subordinate Clause বলে।
Principal vs. Subordinate Clause
SentencePrincipal ClauseSubordinate Clause
I know where he is.I knowwhere he is

সমান মর্যাদাসম্পন্ন দুটি Clause, যেগুলো Co-ordinating Conjunction (and, but, or ইত্যাদি) দ্বারা যুক্ত থাকে, তাদের Co-ordinate Clause বলে। একটি Co-ordinate Clause একটি Principal Clause-এর সঙ্গেও যুক্ত হতে পারে, আবার একটি Subordinate Clause-এর সঙ্গেও।
  • He left the place, but I remained there.
  • Either you must come or I shall go.
  • He is lame and cannot go there alone.
  • I cannot come for I am ill.
  • Read or you will fail.

যে Clause বাক্যে Noun-এর কাজ করে, তাকে Noun Clause বলে। সাধারণত that, বা interrogative pronoun/adverb (what, who, why, when ইত্যাদি) দিয়ে এটি শুরু হয়।
  • I know that he is ill.
  • They say (that) he is dead.
  • That he is ill is known to all.
  • I know what he wants.
  • I shall enquire who did it.
  • I asked him if (or whether) he was there.

The Noun Clause may serve as:

  • (a) Subject of a verb — That he is ill is known to all.
  • (b) Object of a verb — I know when he will come.
  • (c) Object of a preposition — I know nothing of what he will do.
  • (d) Complement to a verb — That is what we expected.
  • (e) In apposition to a noun or "it" — There is a rumour that he is dead.

যে Clause বাক্যে Adjective-এর কাজ করে অর্থাৎ কোনো Noun বা Pronoun-কে বিশেষিত করে, তাকে Adjective Clause বলে।
  • I know the boy who did it.
  • This is the place where he was born.
  • I have lost the book (which) you gave me.
  • This is the reason why he failed.

It is introduced by Relative Pronouns (who, which, that, as) or Relative Adverbs (when, where, how, why, whence, as). The relative pronoun is often omitted when it would stand in the objective case: That is the man (whom) I saw.


A clause used restrictively (defining its antecedent) is Subordinate Adjectival. Used continuatively (adding extra detail, "and he/it/there…") it is Co-ordinate, not Adjectival.
Restrictive (Subordinate Adjectival)Continuative (Co-ordinate)
The book which I bought is lost.My horse, which (= and it) is in the stable, is an Arab.
I know the boys who are present.I saw him yesterday, when (= and then) he gave the news.
I know the place where he lives.I went to your father, who (= and he) promised to help me.

A restrictive clause may be introduced by that; a continuative clause never is. The presence of commas alone does not decide the matter — restrictive clauses may also carry commas.

Note — Sometimes the conjunction is omitted: Had I been rich (= if I had been rich), I would have helped you. Also, who and which make Adverbial clauses of cause or purpose: My brother who (= because he) is ill cannot come.

The nature of a clause depends on the work it does, not on its wording. The same clause can be Noun, Adjective, or Adverbial in different sentences:

  • I know where he lives — Noun clause, object of "know".
  • I know the place where he lives — Adjective clause, qualifying "place".
  • I shall go where he lives — Adverbial clause, qualifying "shall go".
Chapter XXXV

যে বাক্যে মাত্র একটি Subject ও একটি Finite Verb থাকে, তাকে Simple Sentence বলে।
  • I came.
  • I came home yesterday.
  • The good boy obeys his parents.
  • Barking dogs seldom bite.

যে বাক্যে একটি Principal Clause এবং এক বা একাধিক Subordinate Clause থাকে, তাকে Complex Sentence বলে।
Worked example — As you are rich, you must help those who are poor.

Three finite verbs (are, must help, are) → three clauses:

  1. As you are rich — Subordinate Adverbial clause, qualifying "must help".
  2. you must help those — Principal clause.
  3. who are poor — Subordinate Adjective clause, qualifying "those".

যে বাক্যে দুই বা ততোধিক Principal Clause Co-ordinating Conjunction দ্বারা যুক্ত থাকে, তাকে Compound Sentence বলে। Subordinate Clause থাকতেও পারে, না-ও থাকতে পারে।
Worked example — The book which you gave me was a good one, but I have lost it.

Three finite verbs (gave, was, have lost) → three clauses:

  1. The book was a good one — Principal clause.
  2. which you gave me — Subordinate Adjective clause, qualifying "book".
  3. I have lost it — Principal clause.
Note — Co-ordinate clauses are also joined by a Relative Pronoun/Adverb used continuatively (breakable into "and he," "and it," "but he," etc.): I went to the Principal who (= and he) spoke kindly to me.

Contracted sentences avoid repeating a shared word:

  • He came and (he) delivered a lecture. — shared subject
  • They as well as you are wrong. — shared predicate
  • He is poor, but (he is) honest.

When two nouns are joined by and, they usually form one compound subject to a plural verb — the sentence stays Simple, not Compound: He and his friend left the place. Likewise when the subjects are inseparable in meaning: He and I are great friends. Curry and rice is my favourite dish. The sum and substance of his story is this.

When two nouns or phrases are joined by or in a non-alternative sense, they too form a single subject: A tribe or caste is part of a nation — here "caste" is merely another name for "tribe," not an alternative to it.

Note — One authority (M.E.U.) gives "You and I would rather see that angel" as an example of a Compound sentence, but most grammarians treat such inseparable subjects as Simple, per the rule above.

Whether a sentence is Complex or Compound depends on the number of its Principal clauses, not on how many subordinate clauses accompany them. A Complex sentence may still contain co-ordinated subordinate clauses (The boy who came here yesterday and whom you must have seen is my brother); a Compound sentence may still contain several subordinate clauses hanging off its principal clauses. The test is always: are the co-ordinated clauses independent (→ Compound) or subordinate (→ Complex)?

Modern grammarians call a sentence with exactly two principal co-ordinate clauses a Double sentence (He came and played), and one with more than two a Multiple sentence — each co-ordinate clause may carry its own subordinate clauses: He came to the town, stayed for a few days and then returned to his village home.

Note — Some grammarians use the term "Mixed sentence" for one containing both co-ordinate and subordinate clauses, but this is best avoided — it does not settle whether the sentence is fundamentally Complex or Compound.

Adjuncts to the Subject are Adjectives or their equivalents:

  • Adjective — A good boy is loved by all.
  • Participle — Flying clouds are seen in the sky.
  • Participial phrase — The boy playing on the lawn is my brother.
  • Possessive noun/pronoun — My brother is ill.
  • Noun in apposition — Ramesh, my brother, is ill.
  • Preposition with object — A man of principle is liked by all.
  • Adjective Clause — Boys who work hard succeed.
Note — Articles (a, an, the) are strictly attributes, and therefore Adjuncts — though some grammarians simply treat them as part of the subject-word itself rather than listing them separately.

  • Adverb — He speaks well.
  • Adjective used as adverb — He died happy.
  • Adverbial phrase — They walked side by side.
  • Participle — He went away disappointed.
  • Gerundial Infinitive — He came to see me.
  • Preposition with object — We arrived in time.
  • Absolute phrase — The sun having set, we left the place.
  • Adverbial Clause — He went away after I had left.
Note — Adverbial Adjuncts are often confused with Complements. Remember: a word, phrase, or clause that shows how, when, why, or where an action is performed is an Adverbial Adjunct — not a Complement.

Chapter XXXVIII

A quotation is not treated as a Noun Clause — it has no connective linking it to the principal clause, and its wording cannot be altered as a clause's could. Instead, treat the whole quotation as a single noun-equivalent, the object of the reporting verb — so He said, "I am ill" is analysed as a Simple sentence. This rests on four arguments:

  1. A Noun Clause needs a connective (expressed or understood) linking it to the principal clause — no such connective can be supplied before a quotation.
  2. A long quotation may itself contain two or more sentences, which cannot each be related separately to the reporting verb — they must be treated together as a single unit, object of the verb.
  3. A true clause must be able to bend to fit the grammar of the sentence it sits in. A quotation cannot — change its wording and it stops being a quotation, which is exactly why it is marked off with inverted commas.
  4. A quotation sits inside a sentence without joining its grammatical structure. Consider "The accusative of 'I' is 'me'." If the quoted words followed the sentence's own grammar, "of" would force the accusative and the verb "to be" would force the nominative — giving the absurd result "The accusative of 'me' is 'I'." The quotation must therefore stand outside the grammar altogether.
Note — The same logic covers a quoted phrase used as a compound adjective: "I was surprised by his 'I don't care' attitude" has two finite verbs, yet I don't care is not a clause — it is a single fixed unit, and the whole sentence analyses as Simple. If preferred, a quotation may instead be analysed separately on its own, the way a parenthetic clause is (see below).

In "He is the man who, I believe, did it," and "This is the boy who, I think, came the other day," the words I believe / I think can be removed without harming the sentence — they are analysed separately as a Parenthetic Clause, not as Principal or Subordinate.

Parenthetic Phrases work the same way and are likewise analysed apart from the rest of the sentence:

  • He is, to tell the truth, not quite frank.
  • I was, to be frank, much surprised.

  • Introductory "There" (e.g. There is a man here) has lost its force as an adverb of place; it is customarily analysed as an adverbial adjunct, though some treat it separately as "introductory."
  • Interjections (Ah! Alas!) generally stand outside the grammatical structure and are simply labelled "Interjection" — unless the interjection is itself the object of a transitive verb: "Bravo!" shouted the people.
  • Vocatives (Mookerjee, I shall help you) usually stand in apposition to "you," expressed or understood — as in Mookerjee, I shall help you (apposition to "you" expressed) and Karim, go there (apposition to "you" understood).
Note — Some grammarians hold that a Vocative has no grammatical connection with the rest of the sentence at all, and should be left out of the analysis entirely rather than treated as apposition.

  • It … that — distinguish a defining relative clause (It is money that I want) from a conjunction clause in apposition (It is to you that I gave it).
  • It + infinitive — It is easy to say so: "It" may be the formal subject, with the infinitive as the real subject (or in apposition to it).
  • It as formal object — I found it difficult to do the work: "It" is the object of the verb, and the infinitive/clause stands in apposition to it.

Always supply what is understood.

  • He is stronger than I. → Principal: "He is stronger"; Adverbial clause: "Than I (am strong)".
  • He worked harder than ever. → Principal: "He worked harder"; Adverbial clause: "Than ever (he worked hard)".

The conjunctions if, though, till, when, while, whether…or often introduce elliptical Adverbial clauses, with the subject and predicate understood — all of the following remain Complex sentences:

  • I shall certainly join if (I am) appointed.
  • Though (he is) ill, he will attend the meeting.
  • I shall stay on till (I am) relieved.
  • He flared up when (he was) opposed.
  • He fell down while (he was) going up the steps.
  • I shall support you whether (you are) right or wrong.

Clauses introduced by as are frequently elliptical and cause real difficulty. In some cases the ellipsis is so deeply buried that as is best treated as part of a compound conjunction; in others, the missing words should be supplied. Ten representative cases:

#SentenceAnalysis
(a)He acted as before.He acted — Principal. As (he had acted) before — Adverbial clause, qualifying "acted".
(b)He gave the same reply as before.He gave the same reply — Principal. As (he had given) before — Adjective clause, qualifying "reply".
(c)He came as usual.He came — Principal. As (it was) usual (for him to come) — Adverbial clause, qualifying "came". (Or: as = a relative pronoun "which", so "as usual" = "and this was usual" — a Co-ordinate clause.)
(d)I am not such a fool as to do it.I am not such a fool — Principal. As (I should be) — Adjective clause, qualifying "fool" (as = relative pronoun, complement to "should be"). (If I were) to do it — Adverbial clause, qualifying "should be".
(e)Come as soon as you can.Come as soon — Principal. As you can — Adverbial clause, qualifying "as soon". (Or treat "as soon as" as one compound conjunction introducing a single Adverbial clause qualifying "Come".)
(f)Come as soon as possible.Come as soon — Principal. As (it is) possible (for you to come) — Adverbial clause, qualifying "as soon".
(g)Be so good as to help me.Be so good — Principal. As (you should be good) to help me — Adverbial clause, qualifying "so".

"The…the" construction — in The more, the merrier (fully: "By how much more, by so much merrier"), the second clause is treated as Principal. The more he gets, the more he wants works the same way.

Note — Sweet argued the two clauses are so mutually dependent that it is hard to say which is truly principal, and that calling the first one "principal" is a convention justified only by its coming first — a view worth keeping in mind rather than treating the rule as absolute.

Noun Clause as Subject — sentences like That he is right is known, How he went there is known to all, and Whether he will succeed is uncertain are best split two ways:

  • (i) (That he is right) is known — Principal Clause. That he is right — Noun Clause, subject of "is known".
  • (ii) It (that he is right) is known — Principal Clause. That he is right — Noun Clause, in apposition to "It".

(a) Dependent question — a "what"-clause standing for an indirect question is a Noun Clause, object of the verb before it: I ask you what you want. Can you say what he requires? I know what he means.

(b) "What" as a relative pronoun (= "that which") — take Show me what you bought. Three equally valid analyses:

  • (i) Show me — Principal. What you bought — Noun Clause, object of "show".
  • (ii) Show me that — Principal. Which you bought — Adjective Clause, qualifying "that" (since what = that which).
  • (iii) Show me that — Principal. What you bought — Adjective Clause, qualifying "that".

The same three-way analysis applies to This is what I want ("That is that" / "Which I want" / "What I want" qualifying "that").

(c) "What"-clause as Subject — take What he said is true. Four ways to analyse it:

  • (i) (What he said) is true — Principal. What he said — Noun Clause, subject of "is".
  • (ii) It (= What he said) is true — Principal. What he said — Noun Clause, in apposition to "It".
  • (iii) That is true — Principal. Which he said — Adjective Clause, qualifying "That".
  • (iv) That is true — Principal. What he said — Adjective Clause, qualifying "That".

"As soon as" vs. "No sooner…than" — these mean the same thing, but swap which clause is Principal:

Principal clauseSubordinate clause
We startedas soon as the rain stopped.
No sooner did the rain stopthan we started.

"Rather than" — in I will suffer myself rather than (that) he should, "than" functions as a preposition, not a conjunction, and the Noun Clause "that he should (suffer)" is its object.

Ten fully-analysed sentences from the book, clause-by-clause — a model for how the difficulties above apply in practice.

(a) As Isaac grew older, it was found that he had far more important matters in his mind than the manufacture of toys like the little windmill.

Complex sentence.

  1. As Isaac grew older — Adverbial clause, qualifying "was found" in (2).
  2. It was found — Principal clause.
  3. That he had…mind — Noun clause, in apposition to "it" in (2).
  4. Than the manufacture…windmill (was) — Adverbial clause, qualifying "more important" in (3).
(b) The first thing that men learned, as soon as they began to study nature carefully, was that some events take place in regular order and that same causes always give rise to the same effects.

Complex sentence.

  1. The first thing was — Principal clause.
  2. That men learned — Adjective clause, qualifying "thing" in (1).
  3. As soon as…carefully — Adverbial clause, qualifying "learned" in (2).
  4. That some events…order — Noun clause, subject-complement to "was" in (1), co-ordinate with (5).
  5. That same causes…effects — Noun clause, subject-complement to "was" in (1), co-ordinate with (4). Connective: and.
(c) When the children saw him they were so frightened that they all ran away and the garden became winter again.

Double sentence.

  1. When the children saw him — Adverbial clause, qualifying "were frightened" in (2).
  2. They were so frightened — Principal clause, co-ordinate with (4).
  3. That they all ran away — Adverbial clause, qualifying "so" in (2).
  4. The garden…again — Principal clause, co-ordinate with (2). Connective: and.
(d) You have no idea how tired they are at times, but because they do not complain, you think everything is all right.

Double sentence.

  1. You have no idea — Principal clause, co-ordinate with (4).
  2. How tired they are at times — Noun clause, governed by the preposition "of"/"about" understood.
  3. Because they do not complain — Adverbial clause, qualifying "think" in (4).
  4. You think — Principal clause, co-ordinate with (1).
  5. Everything is all right — Noun clause, object of "think" in (4). Connective: but.
(e) The guilty trembled when they came before him, for they knew that his eyes would look straight into their guilt.

Double sentence.

  1. The guilty trembled — Principal clause, co-ordinate with (3).
  2. When they came before him — Adverbial clause, qualifying "trembled" in (1).
  3. They knew — Principal clause, co-ordinate with (1).
  4. That his eyes…guilt — Noun clause, object of "knew" in (3). Connective: for.
(f) When the King came to himself and was alone, pondering over the matter, he saw that the last angel had explained the mystery.

Complex sentence.

  1. When the King came to himself — Adverbial clause, qualifying "saw" in (3), co-ordinate with (2).
  2. (When the King) was alone…matter — Adverbial clause, qualifying "saw" in (3), co-ordinate with (1).
  3. He saw — Principal clause.
  4. That the last…mystery — Noun clause, object of "saw" in (3). Connective: and.
(g) I cannot avoid imagining that, thus reigned by his lessons out of all my suspicion and divested of even all the little cunning which Nature had given me, I resembled, upon my first entrance into the busy and insidious world, one of those gladiators who were exposed without armour in the amphitheatre at Rome.

Complex sentence.

  1. I cannot avoid imagining — Principal clause.
  2. That, thus…cunning, I resembled…gladiators — Noun clause, object of "imagining" in (1).
  3. Which Nature had given me — Adjective clause, qualifying "cunning" in (2).
  4. Who were exposed…at Rome — Adjective clause, qualifying "gladiators" in (3).
(h) Jack was just preparing to force his way through this thicket, when we were startled and arrested by a strange pattering or rumbling sound which appeared to us quite different from any of the sounds we had heard during the previous part of our walk.

Multiple sentence.

  1. Jack was just…thicket — Principal clause, co-ordinate with (2) and (3).
  2. When (= and then) we were startled by a strange…sound — Principal clause, co-ordinate with (1) and (3).
  3. (When we were) arrested (by a…sound) — Principal clause, co-ordinate with (1) and (2).
  4. Which appeared…sounds — Adjective clause, qualifying "sound" in (2).
  5. (That) we had heard…walk — Adjective clause, qualifying "sounds" in (4). Connective: and.
(i) As Captain Lemuel Gulliver had not yet discovered the island of Lilliput, Isaac did not know that there were little men in the world whose size was just suited to his windmill.

Complex sentence.

  1. As Captain…Lilliput — Adverbial clause, qualifying "did know" in (2).
  2. Isaac did not know — Principal clause.
  3. That there…world — Noun clause, object of "did know" in (2).
  4. Whose size…windmill — Adjective clause, qualifying "men" in (3).
(j) How long I might have continued in this torpid state of simplicity I cannot tell, had I not been roused by seeing an old acquaintance whom I knew to be a prudent blockhead, preferred to a place in government.

Complex sentence.

  1. I cannot tell — Principal clause.
  2. How long…simplicity — Noun clause, object of "tell" in (1).
  3. Had I not…acquaintance, preferred to a place in the government (= If I had not been roused…) — Adverbial clause, qualifying "might have continued" in (2).
  4. Whom I knew…blockhead — Adjective clause, qualifying "acquaintance" in (3).

  • I am happy.
  • They were happy.
  • All is well.
Common pitfall — the verb is sometimes wrongly made to agree with the predicate noun instead of the true subject.
